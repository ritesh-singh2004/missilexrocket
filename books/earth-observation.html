Chapter 1: History of Remote Sensing in India - The IRS Programme

India's journey in remote sensing began long before the launch of its first dedicated operational satellite. The seeds of this revolutionary programme were sown in the early 1970s when the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) recognized the immense potential of space-based observations for managing the country's vast and diverse natural resources. The journey from aerial surveys to a sophisticated constellation of Earth observation satellites represents one of the most remarkable success stories in global space technology.

Early Experimental Missions (1975-1987)

The successful launch of Aryabhata on 19th April 1975 by a Soviet Interkosmos rocket from Kapustin Yar marked India's entry into space. While primarily a scientific satellite, Aryabhata demonstrated the technological foundation needed for future Earth observation missions. Following this milestone, two experimental Earth observation satellites were developed: Bhaskara-I (launched 7th June 1979) was India's first experimental remote sensing satellite, carrying a two-band TV camera system and a three-frequency microwave radiometer. Weighing 444 kg, it operated from a near-circular orbit at 534 km altitude. Bhaskara-II (launched 20th November 1981) was an improved version with a three-band TV camera, providing valuable experience in satellite-based resource survey.

The Operational Era Begins: IRS-1A

The launch of IRS-1A on 17th March 1988 from the Soviet Cosmodrome at Baikonur was a watershed moment in India's space history. This was the first indigenous state-of-the-art operational remote sensing satellite, designed and built entirely by India. IRS-1A carried two sophisticated imaging instruments: LISS-I with 72.5 m spatial resolution and 148 km swath, and LISS-II with two separate sensors at 36.25 m resolution each providing a composite swath of 147 km. The satellite operated in a Polar Sun-Synchronous orbit at 904 km altitude, weighing 975 kg with a planned mission life of 3 years (operated until 1992).

IRS Programme Milestones
  • 1988: IRS-1A launched - India enters operational remote sensing era
  • 1991: IRS-1B launched - 11-day revisit with IRS-1A in tandem
  • 1995: IRS-1C introduced multispectral and panchromatic cameras
  • 1999: Oceansat-1 - India's first dedicated ocean observation satellite
  • 2005: Cartosat-1 - India's first dedicated cartographic satellite
  • 2012: RISAT-1 - India's first indigenous SAR satellite
  • 2019: Cartosat-3 achieved 0.25m resolution - highest civilian resolution
  • Current: 11+ operational Earth observation satellites - world's largest civilian EO constellation

Institutional Framework: NNRMS

A unique institutional strategy emerged with the establishment of the National Natural Resources Management System (NNRMS) in 1983, with the Department of Space as the nodal agency. NNRMS integrated traditional survey techniques with remote sensing technology to provide timely, accurate, and specific information on natural resources to decision-makers at all levels. The system evolved into theme-based satellite series covering land/water resources (Resourcesat, RISAT), ocean/atmospheric studies (Oceansat, INSAT, Megha-Tropiques, SARAL), and cartographic applications (Cartosat).

Chapter Summary

India's remote sensing journey progressed through experimental missions (Aryabhata, Bhaskara), culminating in the operational IRS programme beginning with IRS-1A in 1988. The NNRMS framework provided institutional structure, and the programme evolved into theme-based satellite series covering land, ocean, and cartographic applications, making India's IRS the world's largest civilian Earth observation constellation with 11+ operational satellites.

Chapter 2: ISRO Remote Sensing Applications Infrastructure

The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) operates a comprehensive network of centres and facilities dedicated to remote sensing applications. The institutional framework encompasses the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC) in Hyderabad, the Space Applications Centre (SAC) in Ahmedabad, the Indian Institute of Remote Sensing (IIRS) in Dehradun, and various regional centres that collectively form a robust ecosystem for Earth observation data utilisation.

National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC)

NRSC, located in Hyderabad, serves as the primary agency for acquisition, processing, and dissemination of remote sensing satellite data in India. It operates ground stations for receiving data from IRS satellites and other international Earth observation missions. The NRSC Data Centre functions as a one-stop-shop offering a wide range of data products with various resolutions, processing levels, and formats. The centre maintains an extensive archive of satellite imagery dating back decades, serving as an invaluable resource for temporal studies.

Space Applications Centre (SAC)

SAC in Ahmedabad is ISRO's lead centre for payload development and remote sensing application research. It developed the FASAL methodology for crop forecasting and maintains the VEDAS portal for Earth observation data access. SAC has developed specialized software including MIDAS for SAR analysis, INDUS for interferometric SAR, and AVHYAS for hyperspectral data processing.

National Database for Emergency Management (NDEM)

NDEM is a unique geo-portal serving as the national repository of GIS-based multi-scale geospatial database for the entire country. Operational since 2013, it provides space-based inputs in near real-time during disasters and integrates data from multiple forecasting organizations including IMD, CWC, INCOIS, and NRSC. The system includes decision support tools for situational assessment, evacuation planning, and resource management.

Bhuvan: India's Geo-Platform

Bhuvan, developed by NRSC, is India's open data archive and visualization portal providing Earth observation data to public domain users. The portal enables 2D and 3D representation of Earth's surface, supports pest surveillance, disaster services, and high-resolution city imagery. States like Punjab, Karnataka, Himachal Pradesh, and Andhra Pradesh use Bhuvan for applications in forestry, tourism, municipal management, GIS, and geo-tagging. The platform integrates data from Resourcesat, Cartosat, RISAT, and other missions, offering APIs for programmatic access.

Key Infrastructure Statistics
  • Ground Stations: Multiple stations across India for real-time and recorded data reception
  • Data Archive: Petabytes of historical satellite imagery spanning 40+ years
  • Bhuvan Portal: India's own geo-platform with 20+ ministry portals and 30+ state portals
  • MOSDAC: Meteorological and Oceanographic Satellite Data Archival Centre
  • NDEM: National Database for Emergency Management with decision support tools

Chapter Summary

ISRO's remote sensing infrastructure is distributed across multiple centres: NRSC for data acquisition and processing, SAC for payload development and applications research, IIRS for capacity building, and SDSC for launch operations. Together, they form a comprehensive ecosystem including ground stations, data archives, web portals (Bhuvan, MOSDAC), and emergency management systems (NDEM).

Chapter 3: Electro-Optical Satellites - Cartosat Series

The Cartosat series represents India's most advanced fleet of electro-optical Earth observation satellites, specifically designed for high-resolution cartographic and large-scale mapping applications. Developed by ISRO as an extension of the IRS programme, these satellites have been instrumental in enabling precise topographic mapping, urban planning, infrastructure development, and defence applications.

Cartosat-1 (IRS-P5) - The Pioneer

Launched on 5th May 2005 by PSLV-C6, Cartosat-1 was the first dedicated cartographic satellite carrying two panchromatic cameras - one forward-looking (+26 degrees) and one aft-looking (-5 degrees) - enabling near-instantaneous stereoscopic data acquisition with a time difference of only 52 seconds. This unique configuration provided Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) essential for topographic mapping and terrain analysis. The satellite weighed 1,560 kg and operated at 505 km altitude.

Cartosat-2 Series - High Resolution Imaging

The Cartosat-2 series introduced sub-metre resolution capabilities. Cartosat-2 (2007) achieved better than 1 metre panchromatic resolution with 9.6 km swath. These highly agile satellites can be steered up to plus/minus 45 degrees along and across track, enabling 4-5 day revisit capability. The advanced Cartosat-2C (2016) incorporated adaptive optics with 0.65 metre resolution and 60% mass reduction. Cartosat-3 (2019) achieved 0.25 metre resolution with 1,625 kg mass.

Applications and Impact

Cartosat data forms the backbone of India's topographic mapping programme. The Survey of India uses this data to produce and update topographic sheets. Key applications include topographic mapping and DEM generation, urban planning and infrastructure monitoring, rural development and village-level planning, coastal zone management and shoreline change detection, and strategic mapping and border surveillance for defence applications.

Chapter Summary

The Cartosat series has progressed from 2.5m (Cartosat-1) to 0.25m (Cartosat-3) resolution, serving critical roles in topographic mapping, urban planning, infrastructure development, and defence applications, with data products supporting scales from 1:25,000 to 1:2,500.

Chapter 4: Radar Imaging Satellites - RISAT Series

The Radar Imaging Satellite (RISAT) series represents a transformative capability in India's Earth observation programme - the ability to image the Earth's surface through clouds, rain, and in complete darkness. Using Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) technology, RISAT satellites provide all-weather, day-and-night imaging capability essential for monitoring tropical regions like India.

The Need for Radar Imaging

Traditional optical remote sensing satellites rely on reflected sunlight. During India's crucial Kharif (monsoon) cropping season, persistent cloud cover renders optical sensors ineffective precisely when agricultural monitoring and flood assessment are most critical. SAR instruments overcome this limitation by actively transmitting microwave pulses toward the Earth and recording the backscattered energy, enabling imaging regardless of weather or illumination conditions.

RISAT-2 - India's First SAR Satellite

RISAT-2, launched on 20th April 2009, was India's first satellite carrying a SAR payload. Built by ISRO using an X-band SAR manufactured by Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI), this 300 kg satellite was fast-tracked following the 26th November 2008 Mumbai terror attacks. The X-band SAR operated at 9.59 GHz with multiple imaging modes: Spotlight (1m resolution, 10km swath), Stripmap (3m, 40km), Mosaic (1.8m, 60km), and Wide Coverage (8m, 120km).

RISAT-1 - Indigenous C-Band SAR

RISAT-1, launched on 26th April 2012, was India's first fully indigenous radar imaging satellite carrying a C-band (5.35 GHz) SAR payload. This 1,858 kg satellite provided five operational modes with resolutions from 1 to 50 metres and swaths ranging from 10 to 225 km. The C-band frequency was chosen for its superior penetration through vegetation canopy, making it ideal for agricultural monitoring, forestry, and soil moisture estimation.

RISAT-2B and RISAT-2BR1 - Enhanced Resolution

RISAT-2B (May 2019) brought 0.5m x 0.3m resolution capability in an inclined orbit at 557 km. RISAT-2BR1 (December 2019) achieved 0.35 metre resolution, enabling identification of objects separated by just 35 centimetres. These satellites operate in various polarimetric modes and support interferometric applications.

RISAT-1A (EOS-04) - Continuity Mission

RISAT-1A, launched on 14th February 2022, is a continuity mission for RISAT-1 with enhanced capabilities. Weighing 1,710 kg with 2,280W power, it operates in C-band with variable resolution (1-50m) across swath widths of 10-223 km, featuring over 300 Gbit storage capacity and X-band data downlink at 640 Mbit/s.

Chapter Summary

The RISAT series evolved from Israeli-technology-based RISAT-2 (X-band, 2009) to fully indigenous missions like RISAT-1 (C-band, 2012), RISAT-2B/2BR1 (X-band, 2019), and RISAT-1A (C-band, 2022). These satellites provide critical all-weather, day-night imaging essential for agricultural monitoring during monsoon, flood mapping, and defence surveillance.

Chapter 5: Hyperspectral Imaging - IMS-1 & Hypersat

Hyperspectral imaging captures data across hundreds of narrow, contiguous spectral bands spanning the visible, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. While multispectral sensors like LISS capture data in 4-8 broad spectral bands, hyperspectral sensors capture information in 60-200+ contiguous bands, each only 10-20 nm wide. This extraordinary spectral resolution enables identification of materials indistinguishable in conventional satellite imagery.

Principles of Hyperspectral Imaging

Every material on Earth's surface has a unique spectral signature - a characteristic pattern of how it absorbs, reflects, and emits electromagnetic energy across different wavelengths. Hyperspectral sensors exploit this principle to identify specific minerals, vegetation species, soil types, water quality parameters, and man-made materials with remarkable precision.

IMS-1 - India's First Mini Satellite

IMS-1 (Indian Mini Satellite-1), launched on 28th April 2008, was ISRO's first mini satellite demonstrating miniaturized remote sensing capabilities. Weighing just 93 kg, IMS-1 carried three payloads: Mx (36m resolution, 141 km swath), HySI (Hyperspectral Imager with 505m resolution in 64 contiguous bands across 400-950 nm), and LiVHISI. The HySI payload marked India's first operational spaceborne hyperspectral imager.

Applications of Hyperspectral Data

Hyperspectral data enables geological mapping through mineral identification via spectral signatures of iron oxides, clay minerals, and carbonates. In precision agriculture, it detects crop stress and nutrient deficiency before visible symptoms appear. Environmental monitoring includes water quality assessment and oil spill detection. ISRO's SAC has developed AVHYAS (AdVance HYperspectral data Analysis Software) for processing and analyzing hyperspectral data within the QGIS platform.

Chapter Summary

Hyperspectral imaging captures data across hundreds of contiguous spectral bands, enabling identification of specific materials through unique spectral signatures. India's IMS-1 demonstrated spaceborne hyperspectral capability with its HySI payload. Applications span geological mapping, mineral exploration, precision agriculture, and environmental monitoring, with dedicated processing software AVHYAS developed by SAC-ISRO.

Chapter 6: Ocean Satellites - Oceansat & SARAL-AltiKa

India's vast coastline of 7,516 km and extensive Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) spanning over 2 million sq km demand dedicated ocean observation capabilities. ISRO's ocean satellite programme, beginning with Oceansat-1 in 1999, has established India as a significant contributor to global ocean science and monitoring.

Oceansat-1 (IRS-P4) - The Pioneer

Launched on 26th May 1999, Oceansat-1 carried the Ocean Colour Monitor (OCM) with 8 spectral bands in the 402-885 nm range. With 360m spatial resolution and 1,420 km swath, OCM-1 provided revolutionary data for chlorophyll concentration estimation, Potential Fishing Zone (PFZ) identification, and ocean primary productivity studies, serving for over a decade.

Oceansat-2 - Enhanced Capabilities

Oceansat-2, launched on 23rd September 2009, carried three payloads: OCM-2, a Ku-band scatterometer, and Italian ROSA (Radio Occultation Sounder). OCM-2 provides 360m x 236m resolution with 1,420 km swath in 8 spectral bands at 12-bit quantization. The satellite orbits at 720 km in a Sun-Synchronous orbit with 2-day revisit capability.

SARAL-AltiKa - Precision Altimetry

SARAL (Satellite for ARGOS and AltiKa), launched on 25th February 2013, is a landmark Indo-French collaborative mission carrying AltiKa, a Ka-band (35.75 GHz) altimeter provided by CNES (France). AltiKa's Ka-band provides finer spatial resolution and improved accuracy for sea surface height measurement - critical for ocean circulation studies, sea level monitoring, and marine geoid determination.

ScatSat-1 and Oceansat-3

ScatSat-1 (September 2016) provided continuity of scatterometer services for ocean surface wind vector generation. Oceansat-3 (EOS-6, November 2022) continues the ocean colour observation legacy with improved OCM-3 payload, maintaining India's capability for marine resource assessment and climate monitoring.

Chapter Summary

India's ocean satellite programme encompasses ocean colour monitoring (Oceansat series) for fisheries and marine ecosystems, scatterometry (ScatSat-1) for ocean wind mapping and cyclone tracking, and altimetry (SARAL-AltiKa) for precise sea surface height measurement. These satellites collectively support marine resource management, weather forecasting, and climate monitoring.

Chapter 7: Cartographic Applications - Topographic Mapping

Cartographic applications represent one of the primary drivers of India's Earth observation satellite programme. The systematic acquisition of high-resolution satellite imagery has revolutionized the production and updating of topographic maps, enabling the Survey of India to maintain an accurate spatial database of the nation's terrain, infrastructure, and land use patterns.

Topographic Mapping Programme

India's topographic mapping programme leverages satellite data to produce maps at various scales. The traditional ground-based survey approach, while highly accurate, is time-consuming for covering India's 3.28 million sq km land area. Satellite-based cartographic mapping has dramatically accelerated this process:

  • 1:50,000 Scale: Primary national mapping using Resourcesat LISS-III (23.5m) and Cartosat-1 (2.5m)
  • 1:25,000 Scale: Enhanced mapping using Cartosat-2 data for cadastral and infrastructure planning
  • 1:10,000 Scale: High-resolution urban mapping using Cartosat-3 (0.25m) for city-level planning

Digital Elevation Models (DEMs)

Cartosat-1's stereo pair capability enables generation of DEMs with vertical accuracy of 5-10 metres. These DEMs are fundamental for terrain analysis, watershed delineation, flood modeling, road alignment studies, and telecommunications network planning. ISRO has generated national-level DEMs at 30m and 10m resolution using Cartosat stereo data.

Land Information System (LIS)

India is developing a comprehensive Land Information System using satellite-derived cadastral and topographic data. States like Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Andhra Pradesh have pioneered LIS implementation, using high-resolution satellite data for cadastral map modernization and transparent land administration.

Chapter Summary

Cartographic applications form a core mission of India's Earth observation programme, with satellites providing data at resolutions from 36m to 0.25m for mapping scales from 1:1,000,000 to 1:2,500. The systematic approach has enabled comprehensive topographic mapping, DEM generation, and Land Information System development across the country.

Chapter 8: Agricultural Monitoring - Crop Forecasting & FASAL

Agriculture employs over 42% of India's workforce and contributes approximately 18% to GDP. Remote sensing has transformed agricultural monitoring from traditional crop-cutting experiments to sophisticated satellite-based forecasting systems. India's crop monitoring programme is one of the largest and most operationally mature in the world.

Evolution of Agricultural Remote Sensing

Agricultural remote sensing in India began in 1970 with helicopter-mounted multispectral cameras detecting coconut root-wilt disease. The programme evolved through CAPE (1980s-90s), FASAL (2007-present), NADAMS (National Agricultural Drought Assessment), and KISAN programmes.

Mahalanobis National Crop Forecast Centre (MNCFC)

MNCFC, established in 2012 at Pusa, New Delhi, is the operational hub for satellite-based crop forecasting. It integrates remote sensing from SAC Ahmedabad, agrometeorological models from IMD, and econometric models from IEG to produce multiple pre-harvest production forecasts.

Chapter 9: Disaster Management - Flood, Cyclone & Earthquake

India is among the most disaster-prone nations globally, facing floods, cyclones, earthquakes, landslides, droughts, and forest fires. ISRO's Disaster Management Support Programme provides near real-time satellite-based information for all phases of disaster management - prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery.

Flood Monitoring and Mapping

Flood monitoring is one of the most critical applications during India's monsoon season (June-October). NRSC maintains systematic flood monitoring using both optical and radar satellite data. RISAT-1A SAR provides near real-time flood inundation maps regardless of cloud cover, while Resourcesat data supplements mapping during clear weather.

Cyclone Monitoring and Early Warning

India's North Indian Ocean basin is exposed to approximately 10% of the world's tropical cyclones. ISRO's system integrates INSAT-3D/3DR, Oceansat scatterometers, and radar satellites for cyclone detection, intensity estimation, and track prediction. Data products are disseminated to IMD and NDMA for public warnings.

ISRO Disaster Support Statistics (2022-2025)
  • Flood Maps: 319 products generated in 2025 alone
  • Flood Hazard Atlases: Released for 6 states (Assam, Bihar, UP, West Bengal, AP, Odisha)
  • Spatial Early Warning: Operational for Godavari and Tapi rivers
  • International Support: Data provided to 16+ countries under International Charter
  • Turnaround Time: Products within 3-4 hours of satellite data acquisition

Earthquake and Landslide Response

For earthquake response, ISRO provides pre- and post-event satellite imagery for rapid damage assessment. High-resolution Cartosat and RISAT data enable building-level damage mapping. For landslides, NRSC maintains a landslide zonation map using terrain analysis and satellite imagery, providing early warning in vulnerable Himalayan and Western Ghat regions.

Chapter 18: International Cooperation - UNOOSA & Bilateral

India's Earth observation programme operates within a robust framework of international cooperation, contributing to and benefiting from global space-based observation systems. Through bilateral partnerships, multilateral frameworks, and contributions to international disaster response mechanisms, India has established itself as a significant player in the global Earth observation community.

United Nations Committee on Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS)

India is an active member of COPUOS and its subsidiary bodies, contributing to the development of international space law and the peaceful use of outer resources. India signed and ratified the Outer Space Treaty (1967), the Rescue Agreement (1968), the Liability Convention (1972), and the Registration Convention (1976). India's participation in UNISPACE conferences has shaped global policy on Earth observation for sustainable development.

International Charter Space and Major Disasters

India is a founding member of the International Charter Space and Major Disasters, established in 2000. Under this framework, ISRO provides satellite data and value-added products to disaster-affected countries worldwide. India has supported disaster response in over 16 countries including Cambodia, China, Indonesia, Iraq, Japan, Myanmar, Nepal, Oman, Philippines, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, Tajikistan, Thailand, and Vietnam. During events like Cyclone Ditwah in Sri Lanka, ISRO provided extensive SAR and optical satellite data support.

NASA-ISRO SAR (NISAR) Mission

The NISAR mission, launched on 30th July 2025, represents the most significant bilateral partnership in Earth observation. This joint mission between NASA and ISRO is the first dual-frequency (L-band and S-band) SAR satellite, providing unprecedented measurements of Earth's surface changes. NASA provided the L-band SAR, 12m reflector antenna, and high-rate data systems, while ISRO provided the S-band SAR, spacecraft bus, and GSLV launch vehicle.

SARAL-AltiKa - Indo-French Cooperation

SARAL (2013) is a landmark Indo-French collaboration between ISRO and CNES. The satellite carries AltiKa (Ka-band altimeter) from CNES and ARGOS data collection system, with ISRO providing the spacecraft bus and launch. This mission provides critical data for ocean circulation studies and sea level monitoring.

Challenges and the Road Ahead

Despite remarkable achievements, challenges remain. The need for higher temporal resolution for rapid disaster response, geostationary Earth observation for continuous monitoring, and integration of AI/ML for automated analysis represent key priorities. The EOS-09 launch failure in May 2025 highlighted the importance of continued investment in reliable launch vehicles and redundant satellite constellations.

Looking ahead, India's Earth observation programme will continue to evolve with next-generation missions addressing climate change, sustainable agriculture, water security, and urban resilience. The integration of Indian and international satellite data, combined with advanced AI/ML analytics, will create unprecedented capabilities for understanding and managing our planet's resources.

Final Summary

India's Earth observation programme has established the nation as a global leader in civilian remote sensing, with the world's largest constellation of operational EO satellites serving applications from agriculture to defence. The programme's impact on nation building is immeasurable - from enabling food security for 1.4 billion people to providing critical disaster response capabilities. With missions like NISAR and future constellations, India continues to push the boundaries of space-based Earth observation for the benefit of humanity.
