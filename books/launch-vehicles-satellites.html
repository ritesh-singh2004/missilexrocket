Chapter 1

History of Indian Launch Vehicles — SLV-3 to LVM3

India's journey into launch vehicle development began in the early 1970s under the visionary leadership of Dr. Vikram Sarabhai and later Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam. From a small sounding rocket launch in 1963, India has built an indigenous family of launch vehicles capable of deploying payloads ranging from micro-satellites to heavy communication satellites and interplanetary probes.

Key Milestones

  • 1963: First sounding rocket (RH-75) launched from Thumba, Kerala
  • 1979: SLV-3 first test flight (partially successful)
  • 1980: SLV-3 first successful launch — India became 6th spacefaring nation
  • 1987: ASLV first developmental flight
  • 1993: PSLV first flight (unsuccessful debut)
  • 1994: PSLV first successful launch — beginning of the "Workhorse" era
  • 2001: GSLV first developmental flight (D1)
  • 2014: LVM3-X/CARE first experimental flight
  • 2017: GSLV Mk III-D1 first developmental flight with GSAT-19
  • 2022: SSLV first developmental flight

SLV-3: The Pioneer (1979–1983)

Satellite Launch Vehicle-3 (SLV-3) was India's first experimental satellite launch vehicle. It was an all-solid, four-stage vehicle weighing 17 tonnes with a height of 22m, capable of placing 40 kg class payloads into Low Earth Orbit (LEO). The project was conceived and led by Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, who later became the President of India.

SLV-3 employed an open-loop guidance system with a stored pitch programme. The first flight on August 10, 1979 was only partially successful. On July 18, 1980, SLV-3 successfully placed Rohini RS-1 into orbit, making India the sixth member of the exclusive club of space-faring nations.

ASLV: The Bridge (1987–1994)

The Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle (ASLV) was a low-cost intermediate vehicle built on experience from SLV-3. It demonstrated strap-on technology and was designed to place 150 kg class payloads into LEO. ASLV had five flights between 1987 and 1994.

PSLV: The Workhorse (1993–Present)

The Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle was the third generation Indian launch vehicle and the first to be equipped with liquid stages. After its unsuccessful debut in September 1993, PSLV emerged as the most reliable and versatile launch vehicle with over 60 missions and an outstanding success rate.

GSLV: The Cryogenic Challenge (2001–Present)

The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle represented India's entry into heavy-lift capability. Initially using Russian cryogenic stages, GSLV faced numerous setbacks before India successfully developed its own indigenous cryogenic engine.

LVM3: India's Heavy Lifter (2014–Present)

Formerly known as GSLV Mk III, LVM3 is ISRO's heavy-lift launch vehicle with 4,000 kg GTO and 10,000 kg LEO capability. It has completed 7+ successful missions, including Chandrayaan-2 and Chandrayaan-3.

SSLV: The Newcomer (2022–Present)

The Small Satellite Launch Vehicle caters to the growing demand for small satellite launches at 34m tall with 120t lift-off mass.

Chapter Summary

India's launch vehicle programme has evolved from the 40kg-capable SLV-3 to the 4-tonne GTO-capable LVM3 in just four decades. Today, ISRO operates a diverse fleet covering payloads from 10 kg to 10,000 kg.

Chapter 9

Cryogenic Engine Technology — CE-7.5 & CE-20

Cryogenic propulsion represents the highest efficiency rocket engine technology, using liquefied gases stored at extremely low temperatures (below 123K). India's journey to master cryogenic technology spanned over two decades.

What is Cryogenic Propulsion?

Cryogenic propulsion uses liquid hydrogen (LH2, boiling point 20K) as fuel and liquid oxygen (LOX, boiling point 90K) as oxidizer. This combination provides a specific impulse of 450–460 seconds.

Specific Impulse Comparison

  • Solid Propellant (HTPB): 260–275 seconds
  • Earth-Storable Liquid (UDMH/N2O4): 280–315 seconds
  • Semi-cryogenic (LOX/Kerosene): 300–350 seconds
  • Cryogenic (LOX/LH2): 450–460 seconds — Highest efficiency

Chapter 10

Solid Rocket Motor Technology — HTPB Propellant

Solid rocket motors form the foundation of India's launch vehicle programme. From SLV-3's all-solid design to the massive S200 boosters of LVM3, solid propulsion has been ISRO's most proven technology.

HTPB Propellant Composition

HTPB Propellant

  • Fuel: HTPB binder (hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene rubber)
  • Oxidizer: Ammonium Perchlorate (AP) — 68-70% by weight
  • Metal Fuel: Aluminum powder (18-20% by weight)
  • Curing Agent: Isocyanate
  • Properties: Safe to handle, stable during storage, reliable ignition

Design Features

  • High Thrust-to-Weight Ratio: Essential for liftoff

Technological Developments

Key Technologies Under Development

  • Semi-cryogenic Engine (SE2000): LOX/Kerosene, 200t thrust — will replace Vikas in L110
  • C32 Cryogenic Stage: 32t propellant, 22t thrust — enhancing LVM3
  • Reusable Launch Vehicle (RLV): Winged orbital re-entry vehicle
  • NGLV (Next-Gen Launch Vehicle): LOX/Methane partially reusable
  • VTVL Recovery: Vertical takeoff, vertical landing booster technology
  • Space Docking: SPADEX technology for orbital assembly

Cost-Effectiveness

India's space programme is renowned for its cost-effectiveness. Mangalyaan cost just $74 million (less than the movie Gravity), Chandrayaan-3 cost approximately $75 million, and Gaganyaan is budgeted at $2.4 billion for the entire programme including multiple missions. This cost efficiency, combined with reliable launch vehicles like PSLV and LVM3, positions India as a competitive player in the global commercial launch market.

Commercial Space

NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), the commercial arm of ISRO, has secured multiple commercial launch contracts including OneWeb constellation launches on LVM3. The opening of India's space sector to private participation through IN-SPACe has created a vibrant ecosystem with over 200 space startups.

Final Summary

India's space programme stands at a pivotal moment, transitioning from an applications-focused agency to one capable of human spaceflight, planetary exploration, and commercial services. With a robust fleet of launch vehicles, a comprehensive satellite constellation, and ambitious missions ahead, India is poised to become a leading space power in the coming decades. The journey from a sounding rocket in 1963 to landing on the Moon's south pole in 2023 encapsulates the remarkable progress of Indian space science and engineering.
