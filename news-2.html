Launch Vehicle Simulation Network expanded to twelve nodes

14 July 2026

The Launch Vehicle Simulation Network (LVSN) at MissileXRocketSpace has been expanded from eight to twelve compute nodes, significantly increasing its capacity for high-fidelity trajectory modelling. The network is a core component of the Space Research Programme, enabling researchers to simulate complex launch sequences across multiple vehicle configurations simultaneously.

Network Architecture

Each node in the LVSN is equipped with high-performance processors and dedicated simulation accelerators, connected via a low-latency InfiniBand fabric. The distributed architecture allows a single mission simulation to be partitioned across multiple nodes, reducing computation time for complex multi-stage launch profiles from several hours to under 45 minutes.

The four new nodes bring the total simulation throughput to 2,400 core-hours per day, enabling the team to run comprehensive Monte Carlo analyses for mission risk assessment without queuing delays. This is particularly valuable for the upcoming GSLV Mk III mission planning studies, where thousands of trajectory variants must be evaluated.

Simulation Capabilities

The LVSN supports end-to-end launch vehicle simulation from ignition through orbital insertion. Key features include multi-body gravitational modelling for planetary departure trajectories, atmospheric aerodynamics simulation for ascent phase analysis, structural load modelling for vehicle qualification studies, and real-time anomaly injection for abort scenario testing.

The network also integrates with the AI Mission Planning Laboratory, allowing simulation results to feed directly into machine learning pipelines for guidance algorithm development. This tight integration has already produced measurable improvements in trajectory optimisation convergence rates.

Impact on Research

With the expanded network, the Space Research Programme has increased its mission simulation capacity by 50%. Researchers can now evaluate more design alternatives in less time, accelerating the development cycle for next-generation launch vehicle concepts. The team has already identified three trajectory solutions that reduce propellant consumption by 8-12% for geostationary transfer orbit missions.

Student researchers have also benefited from the expansion. Supervised access sessions now support groups of up to six students running independent simulation campaigns simultaneously, compared to the previous limit of three. This has enabled more hands-on learning opportunities and faster progression through the research curriculum.

Next Steps

A further expansion to 16 nodes is planned for early 2027, contingent on funding approval. The additional capacity would support real-time hardware-in-the-loop simulation, where physical flight computers are connected to the simulation network to validate avionics software under realistic conditions.
